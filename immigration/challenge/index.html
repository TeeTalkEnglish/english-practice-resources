Part 3

Know vs. Guess

Understand the difference between knowing, remembering, being unsure, and not understanding.

03
Situation 1

You know approximately when something happened but don't remember the exact date.

Best response:

"I don't remember the exact date, but it was around June."
Why? You give what you genuinely know. You don't invent a specific date.
Situation 2

You don't understand the officer's question.

Best response:

"Could you please repeat the question?"
Why? Asking is better than guessing what they meant.
Situation 3

You understand the question but genuinely don't know the answer.

Best response:

"I'm not sure. I don't know the exact answer."
Why? Honesty is more credible than a made-up answer.
Situation 4

You gave the wrong information by accident.

Best response:

"Sorry, let me correct that."
Why? Self-correction shows honesty, not dishonesty.
Situation 5

You remember something approximately but your memory is fuzzy.

Best response:

"I believe it was 2020, but I'm not completely certain of the year."
Why? You give your best memory and acknowledge the uncertainty.
Situation 6

You understand the question but need a moment to think.

Best response:

"Let me think for a moment."
Why? Pausing shows you're thinking carefully, not rushing to an answer.
Situation 7

You don't understand a specific word or phrase they used.

Best response:

"I'm sorry, I don't know what 'sufficient evidence' means. Could you explain it?"
Why? Asking for clarification prevents misunderstandings.
Situation 8

You remember something but aren't 100% sure of every detail.

Best response:

"I remember we met in 2020. I think it was March, but I might be off by a month."
Why? You give information while being honest about uncertainty.
Part 4

Fix Your Answer

Learn which answers need fixing and what a better answer sounds like.

04
Example 1

Officer:

"When did you get married?"

Bad answer (too uncertain)

"I think it was January 2026... or maybe February... actually I'm not sure."

Better answer

"We got married in January 2026."

Why: You're certain about the month and year. Say it clearly.

Example 2

Officer:

"How do you communicate when you're apart?"

Bad answer (too short)

"Yes."

Better answer

"We usually communicate through text messages and video calls. We talk every evening."

Why: Answer the question directly. Add one useful detail. Then stop.

Example 3

Officer:

"How long have you lived in your current city?"

Bad answer (rambling)

"Well, I moved there about... I think it was 2023? Or maybe the end of 2022? I moved from my parents' house. We had to find a new apartment because my previous apartment was too small and my husband wanted a place closer to his work, which is about 20 minutes away..."

Better answer

"I've lived there for about two years, since 2024."

Why: Short, direct, and clear. Focus on what they asked.

Example 4

Officer:

"What are your plans for work in Australia?"

Bad answer (evasive)

"I'm not sure. I might look for work or I might not. It depends. I haven't really thought about it."

Better answer

"I plan to find work in administration, similar to my current role. I've already started looking at job postings in Australia."

Why: Shows you've thought about your future. Clear and specific.

Example 5

Officer:

"How often do you speak with your family in Vietnam?"

Bad answer (imprecise)

"A lot. Sometimes. Every now and then. I don't know, pretty often I guess."

Better answer

"I call my mother every Sunday. I also message my siblings a few times a week."

Why: Gives specific frequency. Clear and measurable.

Part 5

Follow-Up Pressure

Practice answering increasingly specific follow-up questions without panicking.

05

INTERVIEW CHAIN 1: Communication

How often do you communicate with your husband?
We usually talk every evening through video call.
How long are your typical video calls?
Usually about 30 to 45 minutes.
What do you usually talk about during these calls?
We talk about our work, what we did during the day, our plans for the future, and how we miss each other.
When was your last video call?
Yesterday evening. We talked for about 40 minutes.

Key point: Answer each question separately. Each answer builds on what you said before, but doesn't contradict it.

INTERVIEW CHAIN 2: Finances

Do you and your husband share finances?
Yes. We have a joint bank account for household expenses.
How long have you had this account?
We opened it about two years ago, when we got married.
How much do you typically contribute each month?
I contribute about 1,000 dollars per month. My husband contributes 2,000 dollars.
Can you provide documentation of these transactions?
Yes. I have bank statements from the past 12 months that show our contributions.

Key point: Stay calm and specific. If they ask a follow-up, it doesn't mean you did something wrong—they're just investigating thoroughly.

INTERVIEW CHAIN 3: Daily Life Together

What do you usually do on weekends together?
We usually spend time with his family, go shopping, or visit friends.
How often do you see his family?
We see them most weekends. His parents live about 30 minutes away.
Tell me about a recent weekend you spent with his family.
Last weekend we went to their house for dinner. We cooked together and talked about our plans for moving to Australia.

Key point: Specific, genuine details show authenticity. You don't need to memorize perfect answers.

INTERVIEW CHAIN 4: Travel Together

Have you and your husband travelled together?
Yes. We've travelled together twice.
Where did you go?
We went to Thailand once and to Singapore once.
When did you travel to Thailand?
I believe it was in 2023, around December. We were there for about two weeks.
Who else went on that trip?
It was just the two of us. We wanted to have time alone together.

Key point: Your answers should match your documents (passport stamps, photos, etc.). If you don't remember exactly, say so honestly.

INTERVIEW CHAIN 5: Future Plans

What are your plans for Australia?
We plan to live together as a family, find work, and settle down in a house.
Which city do you plan to live in?
We're planning to live in Melbourne, where my husband has some job connections.
Do you have any commitments or family responsibilities in your home country that might prevent you from settling in Australia?
I visit my family in Vietnam once a year, but I plan to move to Australia permanently. My family understands this.

Key point: Be honest about ties to your home country. Immigration officers know most people maintain connections, but they want to ensure your primary intention is to settle in Australia.

Part 6

Unexpected Question Challenge

Practice answering questions you may not have prepared for. Focus on thinking and answering naturally.

06
Question 1

Officer:

"What did you do together last weekend?"
💡 Think: What did you actually do? Don't invent activities. Simple, real activities are fine.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Start with where you were, then what you did, then how it felt.

Example: "Last weekend we stayed home. We cooked dinner together and watched a movie. It was relaxing."

Question 2

Officer:

"What does your husband usually do after work?"
💡 Think: You should know this. If you don't, that might raise questions about how well you know him.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Describe his typical routine honestly. If he sometimes changes his routine, mention that.

Example: "He usually comes home around 6 PM. Sometimes he goes to the gym, or he relaxes at home and reads. In the evening, we spend time together."

Question 3

Officer:

"What did you have for dinner yesterday?"
💡 Think: This tests whether you're paying attention and remembering real details about your life.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: If you can't remember, say so. If you can, describe it. Simple is fine.

Example: "We had pho for dinner. It's my husband's favorite." OR "I'm not sure exactly. I think we had rice and vegetables, but I don't remember clearly."

Question 4

Officer:

"When did you last speak with your husband's mother?"
💡 Think: You should maintain contact with his family. This is part of the relationship evidence.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Be honest about frequency. If you talk to her regularly, say so. If it's not frequent, that's okay too—just be honest.

Example: "I called her last week. We usually talk every Sunday together when my husband calls her."

Question 5

Officer:

"What is your husband's favorite food?"
💡 Think: Close partners usually know these personal details. Answer naturally.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Keep it simple. A genuine answer is always better than a made-up one.

Example: "He loves pho and anything with beef. He also loves pizza." OR "He's not very picky. He likes most food, but especially seafood."

Question 6

Officer:

"What would you do if your application was refused?"
💡 Think: Be honest but show your commitment to your relationship and plans.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Show commitment to your partner and to Australia, but don't make impossible promises.

Example: "I would be disappointed. My husband and I would discuss our options. We would probably try to understand why and consider reapplying. Our relationship won't change."

Question 7

Officer:

"Do you have any concerns about moving to Australia?"
💡 Think: It's okay to mention minor concerns, but show you're prepared and willing to adapt.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Mention real concerns, but show them as manageable challenges.

Example: "I'm excited about moving, but I'm a little worried about the distance from my family. However, we plan to visit Vietnam once a year. I'm also looking forward to building a new life with my husband."

Question 8

Officer:

"What do you know about life in Australia?"
💡 Think: Show you've researched, but don't try to be perfect. Honest interest is enough.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Mention a few genuine facts and express genuine interest.

Example: "I know that Australia is a large country with many cities. Melbourne is known for culture and arts. I know the cost of living is high, and I'll need to adjust to the climate. I'm excited to experience the culture and make new friends."

Question 9

Officer:

"Can you tell me something that makes you proud about your husband?"
💡 Think: This is a personal question that shows how well you know him and feel about him.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Be specific and genuine. Mention a real quality or achievement.

Example: "I'm proud of how hardworking he is. He has the same job for five years and is very respected at his workplace. He's also very kind to his family and friends."

Question 10

Officer:

"Is there anything else you'd like to tell me about yourself or your relationship?"
💡 Think: This is your chance to say something important that hasn't come up yet.

Take a moment to think of your answer.

Hint: Keep it brief and relevant. Don't introduce new topics. Reaffirm your commitment to your partner and Australia.

Example: "I just want to say that my relationship with my husband is genuine and important to me. I'm really looking forward to building our life together in Australia."

Part 7

Ask for Clarification

Learn when you should ask for clarification instead of guessing.

07
Situation 1

Officer:

"What was the nature of your previous relationship?"

You don't understand what "nature" means in this context.

Situation 2

Officer:

"Can you provide evidence of cohabitation?"

You don't know what "cohabitation" means.

Situation 3

Officer:

"Did you have any bilateral relations in previous years?"

You don't understand this question.

Situation 4

Officer:

"Do you have proof of your bona fides?"

You don't understand this legal term.

Situation 5

Officer:

"What is your health status regarding infectious diseases?"

The question is clear, but you want to be sure they're asking about medical history.

Part 8

Final Mock Interview

Practice a mini interview with 10 realistic questions mixing normal, unexpected, and follow-up questions.

08

Read each question. Think of your answer. Then reveal the answer strategy and example to compare with your own thinking.

Question 1 of 10
How did you and your husband meet?
Take a moment to think. What are the key details?

Strategy: Start with where you met, who introduced you (if anyone), when, and why it mattered.

Example: "We met through a mutual friend in 2022 at a dinner party. My friend knew him from work and introduced us. We talked all evening and discovered we had a lot in common."

How long have you been together?
Be specific about timing. Use "for" + duration or "since" + date.

Strategy: Use present perfect. Include how long together and how long married (if applicable).

Example: "We've been together for two years. We got married in 2024, so we've been married for about one year now."

When was the last time you saw your husband in person?
Give a specific or approximate time. Be honest about the time gap.

Strategy: Be precise about the date or timeframe. Add context if needed.

Example: "Three weeks ago. I visited him for two weeks, and we spent time with his family."

Where does your husband currently live?
Specific address details may be good to have ready.

Strategy: Give the city and state. If asked for the address, provide it from your memory or documents.

Example: "He lives in Melbourne, Victoria. He has a house there with his family."

What does he do for work?
You should know details about his employment.

Strategy: Describe his job, workplace, and how long he's been there.

Example: "He works as a project manager at a construction company. He's been there for three years and really enjoys it."

Could you clarify what your accommodation arrangements will be in Australia?
This is a slightly formal way of asking where you'll live. Show you've thought about it.

Strategy: Show you have a plan. If you don't have specific housing yet, that's okay—just explain your plan to find it.

Example: "We'll live together in Melbourne with my husband's family initially, while we look for our own house. We plan to save and buy a home in about two years."

Tell me what an average day looks like for you and your husband.
This is an unexpected question. Stay calm and describe a real, typical day.

Strategy: Walk through your day from morning to evening. Include work, meals, and time together.

Example: "We wake up around 7. He goes to work, and I work from home. In the evening, we cook dinner together and spend time with his family. We end the day with a video call or relaxing together."

What would happen to your relationship if your visa application was refused?
This is a difficult question designed to test your commitment. Be honest but positive.

Strategy: Show your relationship is strong and independent of visa outcomes. Explain what you'd do next.

Example: "Our relationship wouldn't change. We would be disappointed but we'd discuss our options. We might try again or find another way to be together. My commitment to him is permanent, not dependent on a visa."

I see you mentioned his children in your application. How do you feel about becoming a stepmother?
This shows they've read your application carefully. Give a thoughtful, honest answer.

Strategy: Be honest about your feelings. Show you have a relationship with the children and respect their mother.

Example: "I'm happy about it. I care about his children and want to be a positive part of their lives. I respect their relationship with their mother and my role as a stepmother."

Is there anything else you would like me to know?
This is your final chance to leave a good impression. Keep it brief and relevant.

Strategy: Summarize your key point. Reaffirm your commitment. Don't introduce new major topics.

Example: "I just want to say that my relationship with my husband is genuine and important to me. I'm committed to building our life together in Australia and contributing to society."
